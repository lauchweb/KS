The practice is concerned with looking closely at the things that surround us: images, objects, places, surfaces, gestures, and details that might otherwise pass unnoticed.

Everyday things often carry more than their immediate function. They accumulate traces of use, attachment, memory, and time. A photograph, an object, a fragment, or a familiar image can become a way into something less tangible — a feeling, a memory, or a question that does not have a fixed answer.

The work often begins with this kind of attention. Something catches, lingers, or creates a sense of recognition. From there, images and materials are gathered, altered, combined, and revisited. Meaning is allowed to develop gradually rather than being imposed from the beginning.

Physical and reproduced forms exist alongside one another. Photographs, drawings, paintings, patterns, found elements, and collected material can move between different states. Something once encountered in everyday life can return in another form, carrying traces of where it came from while becoming something else.

Memory and time are present without necessarily being the subject. They appear through worn surfaces, repetition, fragments, alterations, absences, and the distance between an experience and its recollection. The familiar can become uncertain simply by being looked at differently.

The practice remains open to that movement. There is no single medium or fixed method, and no requirement for an idea to remain as it first appeared. Each body of work develops its own language, allowing materials, images, and associations to determine where it goes.

What matters is the possibility of following an idea far enough for it to become something unexpected. The resulting works are not intended to resolve everything they contain, but to leave space for looking, remembering, and finding another way into them.
